In Best Arm Identification (BAI) scenarios we get to sample arms any way we like and our goal is to quickly identify the best arm with high probability. Unlike standard multi-armed bandits, we do not care about the cumulative reward so it is okay to sample suboptimal arms if that allows us to eliminate them more quickly. 

A key object that motivates or even drives various algorithms is the lower bound on the sample complexity of any algorithm. The lower bound is an oracle quantity, meaning it assumes we have access to the true means of the arms. Nevertheless it gives us the optimal distribution from which we should be sampling so if we plug in quantities close to the true means we can get close to the optimal sampling distribution and thus close to the optimal sample complexity. In this post I will show how to quickly compute this lower bound for the case of Bernoulli arms. 

## 1. Setup

There are $$K\ge 2$$ Bernoulli arms with means

$$
\mu=(\mu_0,\mu_1,\dots,\mu_{K-1})\in(0,1)^K.
$$

Assume arm $$0$$ is the unique best arm:

$$
p:=\mu_0>\mu_i=:q_i, \qquad i=1, \dots,K-1.
$$

The case of ties is discussed at the end.

Let

$$
d(a,b) = a\log\frac{a}{b}+(1-a)\log\frac{1-a}{1-b}
$$

be the Bernoulli KL divergence. The fixed-confidence characteristic rate for unstructured Bernoulli best-arm identification is

$$
\Gamma^\star(\mu) = \max_{w\in\Delta_K} \inf_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Alt}(\mu)} \sum_{a=0}^{K-1} w_a d(\mu_a,\lambda_a),
$$

where

$$
\Delta_K=\left\{w\in\mathbb R_+^K:\sum_{a=0}^{K-1}w_a=1\right\},
$$

and the alternative set is

$$
\operatorname{Alt}(\mu) = \{\lambda\in(0,1)^K:\text{arm }0\text{ is not the unique best arm under }\lambda\}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\operatorname{Alt}(\mu) = \bigcup_{i=1}^{K-1} \operatorname{Alt}_i, \qquad \operatorname{Alt}_i=\{\lambda:\lambda_i\ge \lambda_0\}.
$$

Thus

$$
\Gamma^\star(\mu) = \max_{w\in\Delta_K} \min_{1\le i\le K-1} I_i(w),
$$

where

$$
I_i(w)= \inf_{\lambda_i\ge \lambda_0} \sum_{a=0}^{K-1} w_a d(\mu_a,\lambda_a).
$$

The goal is to solve this lower-bound program explicitly up to a one-dimensional scalar equation.

---

## 2. Reduction to pairwise constraints

Fix a challenger arm $$i\ne 0$$. In the infimum defining $$I_i(w)$$, all arms except $$0$$ and $$i$$ can be set equal to their true means at zero cost. Therefore

$$
I_i(w)= \inf_{x\le y} \left\{ w_0 d(p,x)+w_i d(q_i,y) \right\},
$$

where $$x=\lambda_0$$, $$y=\lambda_i$$, and the constraint $$y\ge x$$ has been written as $$x\le y$$.

Since $$p>q_i$$, the unconstrained minimizer $$(x,y)=(p,q_i)$$ violates $$x\le y$$. Hence the constrained minimizer lies on the boundary $$x=y$$. On that boundary, we minimize

$$
w_0 d(p,m)+w_i d(q_i,m)
$$

over $$m\in(0,1)$$. Differentiating gives

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial m} \left[w_0 d(p,m)+w_i d(q_i,m)\right] = \frac{(w_0+w_i)m-w_0p-w_iq_i}{m(1-m)}.
$$

Thus the unique minimizer is

$$
m_i(w)=\frac{w_0p+w_iq_i}{w_0+w_i}.
$$

Therefore

$$
I_i(w) = w_0 d(p,m_i(w)) + w_i d(q_i,m_i(w)).
$$

Using the binary entropy

$$
h(u)=-u\log u-(1-u)\log(1-u),
$$

this can also be written as

$$
I_i(w) = (w_0+w_i)h\left(\frac{w_0p+w_iq_i}{w_0+w_i}\right) - w_0h(p)-w_i h(q_i).
$$

This is the form useful for convex solvers.

---

## 3. Homogeneous parametrization

At any allocation with positive rate, every arm must receive positive mass.

Indeed, if $$w_0=0$$, then choosing $$\lambda_i=q_i$$ and $$\lambda_0\le q_i$$ gives zero cost for challenger $$i$$. Hence $$\min_i I_i(w)=0$$. Similarly, if $$w_i=0$$ for some challenger $$i$$, then choosing $$\lambda_0=p$$ and $$\lambda_i=p$$ gives $$I_i(w)=0$$. Since a strictly positive rate is achieved by any allocation with all coordinates positive, an optimizer must satisfy

$$
w_a^\star>0, \qquad a=0,1,\dots,K-1.
$$

Define the relative challenger weights

$$
r_i=\frac{w_i}{w_0}, \qquad i=1,\dots,K-1.
$$

Then

$$
w_0=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_i}, \qquad w_i=\frac{r_i}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}r_j}.
$$

For each challenger define

$$
m_i(r)=\frac{p+r q_i}{1+r}
$$

and

$$
\psi_i(r) = d(p,m_i(r)) + r d(q_i,m_i(r)).
$$

By homogeneity,

$$
I_i(w)=w_0\psi_i(r_i).
$$

Therefore the original lower-bound program is equivalent to

$$
\Gamma^\star(\mu) = \max_{r_i\ge 0} \frac{\min_{1\le i\le K-1}\psi_i(r_i)} {1+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_i}.
$$

---

## 4. Properties of $$\psi_i$$

For each challenger $$i$$,

$$
\psi_i(0)=0, \qquad \lim_{r\to\infty}\psi_i(r)=d(p,q_i).
$$

Moreover, $$\psi_i$$ is strictly increasing and strictly concave on $$(0,\infty)$$.

To see this, first note that

$$
\frac{d}{dr}m_i(r) = \frac{q_i-p}{(1+r)^2}<0.
$$

Differentiating $$\psi_i$$, the terms involving $$m_i'(r)$$ cancel because

$$
(1+r)m_i(r)=p+r q_i.
$$

Hence

$$
\psi_i'(r)=d(q_i,m_i(r))>0.
$$

Differentiating again,

$$
\psi_i''(r) = \frac{m_i(r)-q_i}{m_i(r)(1-m_i(r))} m_i'(r) < 0,
$$

because $$m_i(r)>q_i$$ and $$m_i'(r)<0$$. Thus $$\psi_i$$ is strictly increasing and strictly concave.

Consequently, for every

$$
z\in(0,d(p,q_i)),
$$

there is a unique number $$r_i(z)>0$$ satisfying

$$
\psi_i(r_i(z))=z.
$$

---

## 5. Equalizer reduction

Define

$$
z_{\max}=\min_{1\le i\le K-1}d(p,q_i).
$$

For each

$$
z\in(0,z_{\max}),
$$

let $$r_i(z)$$ be the unique solution of

$$
\psi_i(r_i(z))=z.
$$

Define

$$
R(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_i(z)
$$

and

$$
\Phi(z)=\frac{z}{1+R(z)}.
$$

The original lower-bound rate equals

$$
\Gamma^\star(\mu)=\max_{0 < z < z_{\max}}\Phi(z).
$$

### Proof

Let $$r=(r_1,\dots,r_{K-1})$$ be any feasible relative allocation and define

$$
s=\min_i \psi_i(r_i).
$$

If $$\psi_j(r_j)>s$$ for some $$j$$, then because $$\psi_j$$ is strictly increasing, we may reduce $$r_j$$ until $$\psi_j(r_j)=s$$. This does not decrease the numerator $$s$$, but it strictly decreases the denominator $$1+\sum_i r_i$$. Hence no maximizer can have unequal values $$\psi_i(r_i)$$.

Therefore every optimizer must satisfy

$$
\psi_1(r_1)=\psi_2(r_2)=\cdots=\psi_{K-1}(r_{K-1})=z
$$

for some $$z\in(0,z_{\max})$$. For this value of $$z$$, necessarily

$$
r_i=r_i(z).
$$

The objective then becomes

$$
\frac{z}{1+\sum_i r_i(z)}=\Phi(z).
$$

Conversely, every $$z\in(0,z_{\max})$$ produces a feasible allocation via $$r_i(z)$$. Therefore maximizing over allocations is equivalent to maximizing $$\Phi(z)$$ over the scalar interval $$(0,z_{\max})$$. $$\square$$

---

## 6. Scalar first-order equation

Because

$$
\psi_i(r_i(z))=z,
$$

implicit differentiation gives

$$
r_i'(z)=\frac{1}{\psi_i'(r_i(z))} = \frac{1}{d(q_i,m_i(r_i(z)))}.
$$

Thus

$$
R'(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}\frac{1}{d(q_i,m_i(r_i(z)))}.
$$

Since

$$
\Phi(z)=\frac{z}{1+R(z)},
$$

we have

$$
\Phi'(z) = \frac{1+R(z)-zR'(z)}{(1+R(z))^2}.
$$

Therefore any interior maximizer satisfies

$$
1+R(z)=zR'(z),
$$

or equivalently

$$
1+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_i(z) = z\sum_{i=1}^{K-1} \frac{1}{d(q_i,m_i(r_i(z)))}.
$$

This is the scalar equation used to compute the optimal information level.

---

## 7. Existence and uniqueness of the scalar root

Define

$$
F(z)=1+R(z)-zR'(z).
$$

The optimality equation is $$F(z)=0$$.

Since each $$\psi_i$$ is strictly concave and strictly increasing, each inverse $$r_i(z)=\psi_i^{-1}(z)$$ is strictly convex. Hence

$$
R''(z)>0.
$$

It is convenient to define

$$
G(z)=zR'(z)-R(z)-1=-F(z).
$$

Then

$$
G'(z)=zR''(z)>0.
$$

Thus $$G$$ is strictly increasing, so $$F$$ is strictly decreasing.

As $$z\downarrow 0$$, we have $$R(z)\downarrow 0$$ and $$zR'(z)\downarrow 0$$, so

$$
F(z)\to 1.
$$

As $$z\uparrow z_{\max}$$, at least one arm $$i$$ with $$d(p,q_i)=z_{\max}$$ has

$$
r_i(z)\to\infty.
$$

For such an arm,

$$
r_i'(z)=\frac{1}{d(q_i,m_i(r_i(z)))}
$$

diverges faster than $$r_i(z)$$ grows, because $$m_i(r)-q_i=O(1/r)$$ and hence

$$
d(q_i,m_i(r))=O(1/r^2).
$$

Therefore

$$
zR'(z)-R(z)\to\infty,
$$

so

$$
F(z)\to -\infty.
$$

Since $$F$$ is continuous and strictly decreasing, there is a unique root

$$
z^\star\in(0,z_{\max}).
$$

This root is the unique maximizer of $$\Phi$$.

---

## 8. Putting everything together

Let $$z^\star$$ be the unique solution of

$$
1+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_i(z) = z\sum_{i=1}^{K-1} \frac{1}{d(q_i,m_i(r_i(z)))}.
$$

For each challenger $$i$$, let $$r_i^\star=r_i(z^\star)$$, where

$$
\psi_i(r_i^\star)=z^\star.
$$

Then the optimal allocation is

$$
w_0^\star= \frac{1}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_i^\star},
$$

and

$$
w_i^\star= \frac{r_i^\star}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}r_j^\star}, \qquad i=1,\dots,K-1.
$$

The optimal lower-bound rate is

$$
\Gamma^\star(\mu) = w_0^\star z^\star = \frac{z^\star}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}r_i^\star}.
$$

Equivalently, every challenger constraint is active:

$$
I_i(w^\star)=\Gamma^\star(\mu), \qquad i=1,\dots,K-1.
$$

## 10. Interpretation

The solution is an equalizer allocation. The best arm receives weight $$w_0$$, and each challenger receives just enough relative weight $$r_i=w_i/w_0$$ to make its pairwise confusion cost equal to the common bottleneck level.

A very close challenger has small $$d(p,q_i)$$, so its function $$\psi_i$$ rises slowly and it needs large $$r_i$$. A far challenger has large $$d(p,q_i)$$, so its function $$\psi_i$$ rises quickly and it needs much less mass.

This explains why, for means such as

$$
0.40, \quad 0.39, \quad 0.38,
$$

the top two arms receive most of the allocation. The gap from $$0.40$$ to $$0.38$$ is twice the gap from $$0.40$$ to $$0.39$$, and locally Bernoulli KL scales quadratically in the gap. Thus the $$0.38$$ arm is roughly four times easier to rule out than the $$0.39$$ arm, so it needs much less direct sampling even though its mean looks visually close to the best arm.

## 11. Ties

If there are ever ties between any arms, just work with the unique arm means then split the optimal allocation among the tied arms.
