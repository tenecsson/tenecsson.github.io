In the previous post we saw how to quickly compute the best arm identification lower bound, and the corresponding asymptotically optimal sampling strategy.  

Sometimes we are interested in finding the top $$m$$ arms instead of just the best arm. In this post I propose a fast approximation for the top-m identification lower bound in Bernoulli bandits. The accompanying code shows that this approximation is very accurate in practice.

Throughout, arm $$i$$ has Bernoulli mean $$\mu_i \in (0,1)$$, and

$$
d(p,q) = p \log \frac{p}{q}   + (1-p)\log \frac{1-p}{1-q}
$$

denotes Bernoulli KL divergence.

## Best-Arm Identification Refresher

Suppose there are $$K$$ arms and a unique best arm. After relabeling, assume

$$
\mu_1 > \mu_2 \ge \cdots \ge \mu_K.
$$

For the purposes of the lower bound we can assume that the means are strictly ordered, i.e. $$\mu_1 > \mu_2 > \cdots > \mu_K$$. This is because if there are ties, we can run the algorithm on the unique means and then split the allocation evenly among tied arms. This does not change the lower bound.

The fixed-confidence lower bound is governed by an information game. The
algorithm chooses asymptotic sampling proportions

$$
w \in \Delta_K
  := \left\{w \in \mathbb{R}_+^K : \sum_{i=1}^K w_i = 1\right\},
$$

and nature chooses an alternative bandit instance in which arm $$1$$ is no
longer the best arm. The lower-bound exponent is

$$
\Gamma^\star
= \max_{w \in \Delta_K}
  \inf_{\lambda : \operatorname{argmax}_i \lambda_i \ne 1}
  \sum_{i=1}^K w_i d(\mu_i,\lambda_i).
$$

For best-arm identification, the relevant alternatives are pairwise: for each
challenger $$j \ne 1$$, nature only needs to make arm $$j$$ tie arm $$1$$.
All other arms can remain unchanged. Thus the inner problem for challenger
$$j$$ is

$$
\inf_x  w_1 d(\mu_1,x)  + w_j d(\mu_j,x).
$$

Define the pairwise confusion cost

$$
C_{1j}(w_1,w_j) := \inf_x   w_1 d(\mu_1,x)  + w_j d(\mu_j,x).
$$

For Bernoulli arms, the optimizer is the weighted average

$$
x^\star
= \frac{w_1 \mu_1 + w_j \mu_j}{w_1+w_j}.
$$

So the best-arm lower-bound exponent can be written as

$$
\Gamma^\star_{\mathrm{BAI}} = \max_{w \in \Delta_K} \min_{j \ne 1} C_{1j}(w_1,w_j).
$$

The important structural fact is that each constraint involves only two arms: the best arm and one challenger.

## Top-m Identification

Now suppose the goal is not to identify the single best arm, but the set of
the top $$m$$ arms. Relabel the arms so that

$$
\mu_1 \ge \cdots \ge \mu_m > \mu_{m+1} \ge \cdots \ge \mu_K.
$$

Let

$$
T = \{1,\ldots,m\},
\qquad
B = \{m+1,\ldots,K\}
$$

be the true top and bottom sets. A wrong answer occurs when some bottom arm enters the top set and some top arm leaves it. The most local way for this to happen is to make a top arm $$i \in T$$ tie a bottom arm $$j \in B$$. This gives the same pairwise cost

$$
C_{ij}(w_i,w_j) :=
\inf_x   w_i d(\mu_i,x) + w_j d(\mu_j,x), \qquad i \in T,\ j \in B.
$$

Therefore the top-m lower-bound exponent is

$$
\Gamma^\star_{\mathrm{top}\text{-}m} = \max_{w \in \Delta_K} \min_{i \in T,\ j \in B} C_{ij}(w_i,w_j).
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{maximize} \quad & \gamma \\
\text{subject to} \quad
& \sum_{k=1}^K w_k = 1, \\
& w_k \ge 0, \\
& \gamma \le C_{ij}(w_i,w_j),
  \qquad i \in T,\ j \in B.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a convex optimization problem in the usual hypograph form because $$C_{ij}$$ is concave in $$(w_i,w_j)$$. But it has

$$
m(K-m)
$$

pairwise constraints. That is fine for small instances, but it is more than we
want if this lower bound is being evaluated repeatedly inside a larger
algorithm.

## The Boundary Arms

The top-m problem has two special arms:

$$
a := m,
\qquad
b := m+1.
$$

Arm $$a$$ is the weakest true top arm, and arm $$b$$ is the strongest true
bottom arm. They define the decision boundary.

The full top-m lower bound protects against every top-bottom swap. But many of those swaps are far from the boundary. If $$i$$ is a very strong top arm and $$j$$ is a very weak bottom arm, confusing $$i$$ and $$j$$ is usually much easier to rule out than confusing arms near the boundary.

This motivates the double-star approximation.

## The Double-Star Approximation

The double-star edge set is

$$
E_{\mathrm{ds}} = \{(i,b): i \in T\} \cup \{(a,j): j \in B\}.
$$

In words:

- compare every top arm to the strongest bottom arm $$b$$;
- compare the weakest top arm $$a$$ to every bottom arm.

This keeps the two stars centered at the boundary arms and includes the central boundary edge $$(a,b)$$. The double-star lower-bound approximation is

$$
\Gamma^\star_{\mathrm{ds}} = \max_{w \in \Delta_K} \min_{(i,j) \in E_{\mathrm{ds}}} C_{ij}(w_i,w_j).
$$

Since $$E_{\mathrm{ds}}$$ is a subset of all top-bottom edges, the double-star program has fewer constraints:

$$
|E_{\mathrm{ds}}|
= m + (K-m) - 1
= K-1.
$$

The approximation is optimistic as a constrained optimization problem: fewer constraints can only increase the best constrained value. But it is often a good approximation because every omitted edge $$(i,j)$$, with $$i \ne a$$ and $$j \ne b$$, is separated from the boundary on both sides:

$$
\mu_i \ge \mu_a > \mu_b \ge \mu_j.
$$

The retained edges are the nearest-neighbor obstructions against the two ways the top-m set can fail:

- a top arm must be distinguishable from the strongest bottom arm;
- a bottom arm must be distinguishable from the weakest top arm.

Empirically, these boundary comparisons tend to dominate the allocation.

## Homogeneous Mass Form

The one-dimensional reduction starts from a homogeneity trick. For any
$$\alpha > 0$$,

$$
C_{ij}(\alpha s,\alpha t)
= \alpha C_{ij}(s,t).
$$

This follows immediately from the definition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_{ij}(\alpha s,\alpha t) &=
\inf_x \alpha s d(\mu_i,x) + \alpha t d(\mu_j,x) \\ &= \alpha \inf_x s d(\mu_i,x) + t d(\mu_j,x).
\end{aligned}
$$

Using this homogeneity, rewrite the simplex problem as an unnormalized mass problem. Instead of maximizing $$\gamma$$ over weights $$w$$, minimize total mass $$Z$$ over variables $$z_k \ge 0$$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{minimize} \quad
& Z = \sum_{k=1}^K z_k \\
\text{subject to} \quad
& C_{ij}(z_i,z_j) \ge 1,
  \qquad (i,j) \in E_{\mathrm{ds}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The two formulations are equivalent by the change of variables

$$
z = \frac{w}{\gamma},
\qquad
w = \frac{z}{\sum_k z_k},
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{\sum_k z_k}.
$$

Thus we can solve the mass problem and recover the lower-bound exponent as the reciprocal of the total mass.

## Eliminating the Non-Boundary Arms

Let

$$
x := z_a,
\qquad
y := z_b
$$

be the unnormalized masses on the two boundary arms.

Now fix $$x$$ and $$y$$. In the double-star edge set, a non-boundary top arm
$$i \in T \setminus \{a\}$$ appears in exactly one constraint:

$$
C_{ib}(z_i,y) \ge 1.
$$

Since $$z_i$$ contributes positively to the objective, its optimal value is
the smallest feasible one. Define

$$
u_i(y)
:=
\inf\{u \ge 0 : C_{ib}(u,y) \ge 1\}.
$$

Similarly, a non-boundary bottom arm $$j \in B \setminus \{b\}$$ appears in
exactly one constraint:

$$
C_{aj}(x,z_j) \ge 1.
$$

Its optimal value is

$$
v_j(x)
:=
\inf\{v \ge 0 : C_{aj}(x,v) \ge 1\}.
$$

Therefore, after fixing the boundary masses, every other mass is determined independently.

Define the two side costs

$$
A(x) = x + \sum_{j \in B \setminus \{b\}} v_j(x),
$$

and

$$
B(y) = y + \sum_{i \in T \setminus \{a\}} u_i(y).
$$

The double-star mass problem reduces to the two-variable problem

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{minimize} \quad
& A(x) + B(y) \\
\text{subject to} \quad
& C_{ab}(x,y) \ge 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is already a large simplification: all $$K-2$$ non-boundary masses have been eliminated by one-dimensional inversions.

## Computing the One-Edge Inversions

The functions $$u_i(y)$$ and $$v_j(x)$$ are cheap to evaluate because of
homogeneity.

For a top non-boundary arm,

$$
C_{ib}(u,y) = y C_{ib}\left(\frac{u}{y},1\right).
$$

Let $$r = u/y$$. Then $$u_i(y) = y r_i(y)$$, where $$r_i(y)$$ solves

$$
C_{ib}(r_i(y),1) = \frac{1}{y}.
$$

Similarly,

$$
C_{aj}(x,v) = x C_{aj}\left(1,\frac{v}{x}\right).
$$

Let $$s = v/x$$. Then $$v_j(x) = x s_j(x)$$, where $$s_j(x)$$ solves

$$
C_{aj}(1,s_j(x)) = \frac{1}{x}.
$$

Each equation is scalar and monotone in the unknown ratio, so it can be solved by bisection or Brent root finding.

## Reducing to One Dimension

The remaining coupling is the central boundary constraint

$$
C_{ab}(x,y) \ge 1.
$$

**Feasibility domain for $$x$$.** For a fixed $$x$$, define the smallest boundary-bottom mass satisfying the central edge:

$$
Y_c(x)
:=
\inf\{y \ge 0 : C_{ab}(x,y) \ge 1\}.
$$

This value is finite if and only if

$$
x d(\mu_a,\mu_b) > 1,
$$

because

$$
\lim_{y \to \infty} C_{ab}(x,y)
= x d(\mu_a,\mu_b).
$$

So the domain for $$x$$ is

$$
x > x_{\min}
:=
\frac{1}{d(\mu_a,\mu_b)}.
$$

**Feasibility domain for $$y$$.** The boundary-bottom mass $$y$$ also has a feasibility lower bound coming from the top-star inversions. Each non-boundary top arm $$i \in T \setminus \{a\}$$ requires

$$
C_{ib}(u_i,y) \ge 1,
$$

and this equation has a finite solution $$u_i(y)$$ only when $$y$$ is large enough that the constraint is achievable. Specifically,

$$
\lim_{u \to \infty} C_{ib}(u,y) = y\, d(\mu_b,\mu_i),
$$

so we need $$y\, d(\mu_b,\mu_i) > 1$$, i.e.\ $$y > 1/d(\mu_b,\mu_i)$$. Taking the tightest of these gives the feasibility threshold

$$
y_{\min} := \max_{i \in T \setminus \{a\}} \frac{1}{d(\mu_b,\mu_i)}.
$$

**Convexity and the unconstrained minimizer.** On the feasible domain $$y > y_{\min}$$, the function $$B$$ is convex. One way to see this: for each top-star edge,

$$
\{(u,y): C_{ib}(u,y) \ge 1\}
$$

is a convex set because $$C_{ib}$$ is concave as an infimum of linear functions of $$(u,y)$$, and superlevel sets of concave functions are convex. Since this set is the epigraph of $$u_i(y)$$, the function $$u_i$$ is convex. Hence $$B$$, being a sum of convex functions plus $$y$$, is convex.

Let

$$
y_0 \in \operatorname*{argmin}_{y > y_{\min}} B(y)
$$

be the unconstrained minimizer of the top side over its feasible domain. Then the best boundary-bottom mass for a fixed $$x$$ is

$$
y(x) = \max\{Y_c(x),\; y_{\min},\; y_0\}.
$$

The three-way max reflects three constraints on $$y$$: the central edge, the top-star feasibility domain, and the convex optimality of the top side.

Therefore the whole double-star approximation reduces to a single scalar minimization:

$$
\min_{x > x_{\min}}
\left[
  A(x) + B(y(x))
\right].
$$

After finding the best $$x^\star$$, set

$$
y^\star = y(x^\star),
$$

then reconstruct all masses:

$$
z_a = x^\star,
\qquad
z_b = y^\star,
$$

$$
z_i = u_i(y^\star),
\qquad i \in T \setminus \{a\},
$$

and

$$
z_j = v_j(x^\star),
\qquad j \in B \setminus \{b\}.
$$

Finally normalize:

$$
w_k^\star = \frac{z_k}{\sum_{\ell=1}^K z_\ell}, \qquad \Gamma^\star_{\mathrm{ds}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{\ell=1}^K z_\ell}.
$$

This is the exact one-dimensional reduction for the double-star edge set.

## Algorithm Sketch

The solver can be summarized as follows.

1. Sort arms by decreasing mean and identify the boundary arms $$a=m$$, $$b=m+1$$.
2. Precompute the functions $$u_i(y)$$ and $$v_j(x)$$ as scalar root-finding problems.
3. Compute $$y_{\min} = \max_{i \in T \setminus \{a\}} 1/d(\mu_b, \mu_i)$$ and find the unconstrained minimizer $$y_0$$ of $$B(y)$$ over $$y > y_{\min}$$.
4. For a candidate $$x > 1/d(\mu_a,\mu_b)$$:
   - compute $$Y_c(x)$$ by scalar root finding;
   - set $$y(x)=\max\{Y_c(x),\, y_{\min},\, y_0\}$$;
   - evaluate $$A(x)+B(y(x))$$.
5. Minimize this one-dimensional objective over $$x$$.
6. Reconstruct the masses and normalize.

In code, it is numerically convenient to search over an unconstrained real parameter $$t$$ via

$$
x = x_{\min}(1+\exp t).
$$

This automatically respects the feasibility domain.

## Why This Is Useful

The full top-m lower bound has $$m(K-m)$$ constraints. The double-star approximation keeps only $$K-1$$ constraints, and the special star structure allows all non-boundary arms to be eliminated analytically up to scalar inversions. The remaining optimization is one-dimensional.

The approximation is motivated by the geometry of the top-m decision boundary: the weakest top arm and strongest bottom arm mediate the most important confusions. The one-dimensional reduction then exploits the fact that, in the double-star graph, every non-boundary arm participates in only one constraint.

The result is a fast lower-bound approximation that still retains the information-theoretic pairwise KL geometry of the original problem.
